Five Questions Every Growing Business Should Ask

Growth is easier to manage when leaders regularly test customer value, economics, operating capacity, cash and focus.

Growth creates opportunity, but it also amplifies weaknesses. A small inconsistency can become expensive when transaction volumes rise, a founder becomes a bottleneck or customers expect a level of delivery the operating model cannot yet support. These five questions help leaders test whether growth is becoming more sustainable.

1. Who is our most valuable customer—and why?

A business cannot serve every customer equally well. Identify the customer groups whose needs fit your strengths, who value your offer and whose economics support sustainable delivery. Look beyond revenue to margin, service intensity, retention and referral potential.

Use customer conversations and transaction data to test whether your assumed value proposition matches what buyers actually care about.

2. Do the economics improve as we grow?

Revenue alone cannot tell you whether growth creates value. Understand gross margin, acquisition cost, service cost, discounts, payment delays and the resources needed to support each customer.

Build a simple model showing what happens to contribution and cash when volume, price, costs or collection time change.

3. Can our operating model deliver the promise?

Map the work from enquiry to delivery and after-sales support. Identify manual bottlenecks, single points of failure, unclear ownership and quality risks. Growth plans should include the people, processes, systems and capacity needed to keep promises.

If founders must personally approve every decision, document the decisions that can safely move closer to the work.

4. Do we have enough cash and control?

Profitable activity can still create a cash squeeze when customers pay late, inventory is purchased early or expansion costs arrive before collections. Track cash timing and commitments alongside the income statement.

Set basic controls for approvals, reconciliations, credit decisions, tax obligations and financial reporting. Good controls protect growth; they are not merely bureaucracy.

5. What should we stop doing to focus?

Every new product, customer segment and channel consumes attention. Review work that is low-margin, distracting or inconsistent with strategic direction. A deliberate stop-doing list can release capacity for the opportunities that matter most.

Review these questions on a regular cadence, not only when performance weakens. The purpose is to spot pressure points early enough to act.

Key takeaways

  • Identify customer groups where the business creates distinct value.
  • Track contribution and cash, not just sales.
  • Prepare people and processes for higher volume.
  • Strengthen financial discipline as complexity increases.
  • Protect focus by stopping low-value work.
